I read *Persian Fire* as preparation for my marathon in Athens: the Athens Marathon. The Authentic. I figured if I'm going to run this ancient route, I should know what I'm actually doing. I should know what I'm actually running on. So I started it about a week and a half before I went, and I finished it in the middle of the marathon.

So what is *Persian Fire* actually about? I'll give you two layers.

On the first level, *Persian Fire* is about the Persian Wars between the Greeks and the Persians. It's in roughly three sections: the rise of Persia, the history of Athens and Sparta before the war, and then the war itself.

On the second level, the book is low-key about the United States and the War on Terror. Tom Holland seems to really enjoy making these references where he continually calls Persia the superpower. The Persian superpower is monotheistic and they really love God, whereas the Greeks are barbarians. They're polytheistic. They're insurgents. So there's this funny role-switching happening. The Greeks are who we consider the foundational culture of Western civilization, and the Persians are this alien power from our past. But the Greeks are functionally the Taliban relative to the Persians, at least at the time. The Greeks are, not to bring the point home, racist, and they don't really understand the wider world, whereas the Persians are multicultural, integrating different places. They're cosmopolitan. The Greeks are sexist against the Persians because they do things that are a little gay, like wearing trousers. So you have this funny dichotomy that Tom doesn't always explicitly say, but he is referencing.

In terms of what stuck with me: I understood that the Battle of Marathon was this important moment in Western civilization, but I didn't really understand anything outside of the marathon itself. That it was this stand against the Persians, making sure that Greece was able to stay independent, was something I didn't understand. As I was riding up to Marathon on a bus filled with a bunch of other people, all full of anxiety, and then running back down, it didn't make me feel like I was there, but it helped me understand the land I was crossing over.

The history of Persia is something that I don't think I know that much about. I learned, post-reading this, that you're not really supposed to call them the Persians anymore. Basically everything you put into Google will correct it to the Achaemenid Empire. I really just think we should call them Persia. But that's one interesting tidbit.

I'll probably come back to read more about this. This is kind of the first action of the Iron Age, and I'd like to learn more about what was actually happening in the Bronze Age.
