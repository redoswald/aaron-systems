I have an entire history list of China that I will continue to do over the course of this year. And it makes sense first to start with a high-level overview.

I just finished *Breakneck* a couple months ago. I understand where China is at now with its technological prowess, set to reach parity with the United States. But I fundamentally want to be able to understand why there was a Great Divergence with China in the first place, and then to also understand why China has been able to catch up so functionally, so well, relative to other places.

And so now we have to go all the way back and figure out, okay, what has happened since the beginning?

And *The Story of China* tells the story of China. It goes through all the dynasties. It brings back to mind the AP World History class that I took when I was 14, and it tells it in a matter-of-fact way.

I understand that he also has a documentary where he tells it more by traveling, going places, and seeing things. But the book mostly tells it in a textbook format.

We go through the Xi and Shang empires at the beginning, though maybe empire is not the right word. We go into the Han, the Tang, the Song, the Yuan, the Ming, the Qing, and then finally the Communist Party, or the Republic, then the Communist Party.

*(AI note: The conventional sequence begins with Xia, Shang, Zhou, Qin, and Han, followed by several periods of division and reunification before Sui, Tang, Song, Yuan, Ming, and Qing. The list above captures most of the major later dynasties but skips Zhou, Qin, Sui, and the intervening periods. The Republic of China preceded the People's Republic of China, established in 1949.)*

So that was a fresh reminder. There was a mnemonic we had in high school that I don't remember because it was about monkeys, and I feel like maybe we shouldn't have had a mnemonic about China and monkeys to remember the dynasties.

But some things that stuck with me were the cultural elements, and how there were a couple of impressions that I had that I think were wrong.

I kind of had this concept of a Hegelian theory of China that I maybe got from reading the big histories of the Middle East and the gunpowder empires: that outside of the democratic nation-states in Europe, there is often this succession power struggle. So I got this concept of the succession crisis in empires, that these succession crises were mostly gunpowder things. Turns out that's not true.

It turns out in China that while there are succession crises, the primary things that cause dynasties to fall are outside invaders just replacing the leader, or internal peasant revolts. Both of these are generally preceded by a great, terrible set of natural disasters that just destabilize things.

So there's a lot more cultural continuity. One empire really does follow after the other.

And so while understanding more of China's history doesn't necessarily make me think about this concept of the non-Western rise and fall of hydraulic succession empires, it does make me see the specialness of China in terms of a long history.

We also read about this in *Escape from Rome*, where the continuity of China is really different. China falls apart, it comes back together. That's not the same thing that happened in Europe, and it's vaguely also not, but sometimes is, what happens in the other mega-regions of the Earth.

The minor, or not minor, but middle dynasties are actually very interesting in the text. There are periods in China of what may be considered broader intellectual growth.

The relationship between the Tang Dynasty and Buddhism is not something I was aware of. Hearing about the guy who traveled from China through Tibet into India and brought back essentially all these Buddhist documents is a really fascinating story.

*(AI note: This is Xuanzang, the seventh-century Chinese Buddhist monk who traveled overland to India and returned with hundreds of Buddhist texts, which he helped translate into Chinese. His route ran through Central Asia along the Silk Road rather than through Tibet.)*

I'm not really sure of anything in the West that has a similar kind of resonance, of a guy just wanting knowledge and bringing it back to his home. It's kind of like a hero's journey that happens in real life.

And yeah, it gives me a lot to think about.
